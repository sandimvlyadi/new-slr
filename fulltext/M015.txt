Abstract

Artificial intelligence (AI) has shown promise in detecting and characterizing musculoskeletal diseases

from radiographs. However, most existing models remain task-specific, annotation-dependent, and

limited in their adaptability across diseases and anatomical regions. Although a comprehensive

foundation model trained on large-scale musculoskeletal radiographs is clinically needed, publicly

available datasets remain limited in size and lack sufficient diversity to enable training across a wide

range of musculoskeletal conditions and anatomical sites. Here, we present SKELEX, a large-scale
                                                                      S
                                                                ES
foundation model for musculoskeletal radiographs, trained using self-supervised learning on 1.2 million

                                                         PR
diverse, condition-rich images. The model was evaluated on 12 downstream diagnostic tasks and

generally outperformed baselines in fracture detection, osteoarthritis grading, and bone tumor
                                                 IN
classification. Furthermore, SKELEX demonstrated unsupervised reconstruction-based anomaly
                                         LE
localization, producing error maps that identified pathologic regions without task-specific training.
                                      C
Building on this capability, we developed an interpretable, region-guided bone tumor classifier. This
                                TI
model maintained robust performance on independent external datasets and was deployed as a publicly
                             R
                         A
accessible web application, serving as a proof of concept for its potential clinical translation. Overall,

SKELEX provides a scalable, label-efficient, and broadly applicable AI framework for musculoskeletal

radiographs, with its robust external validity specifically demonstrated in bone tumor applications.

Introduction

Musculoskeletal (MSK) disorders affect more than 1.7 billion people worldwide, representing a major

global health challenge1,2. Unlike other organ systems that are dominated by a few disease categories,

the MSK system encompasses a remarkably diverse spectrum of disorders, ranging from acute fractures

and degenerative arthritis to inflammatory, metabolic, infectious, developmental, and malignant

conditions3. This diversity introduces a dual layer of diagnostic complexity, as each condition may

present variable manifestations across numerous anatomical regions within the musculoskeletal system.
                                                                     S
                                                              ES
Accurate interpretation of radiographs, the first-line imaging modality for most MSK disorders, is

                                                        PR
therefore highly dependent on expert experience4,5. However, the global shortage of trained

radiologists6,7, combined with the increasing demand for imaging2, is increasing diagnostic
                                                IN
inconsistency and contributing to delays in patient care.
                                        LE
These diagnostic challenges underscore the need for scalable computational tools capable of assisting
                                     C
                               TI
image interpretation across diverse diseases and anatomical regions8. Indeed, artificial intelligence (AI)

                            R
has shown significant promise in augmenting radiographic interpretation, particularly in well-defined
                         A
tasks such as traumatic fracture detection9–12 and osteoarthritis grading13. Nonetheless, existing MSK AI

systems remain narrowly specialized, typically trained on small, single-task datasets that rely on expert

annotations14–19. Models designed for fracture detection often fail when encountering tumors or

infections in the same image, and tools optimized for one body region rarely transfer to another. This

fragmentation has hindered clinical adoption despite increasing enthusiasm for AI-assisted

musculoskeletal care.

Foundation models (FMs) offer a transformative path forward. FMs are AI models trained on large,
                                        ARTICLE IN PRESS




diverse datasets to learn transferable patterns rather than being confined to a single task. By learning

comprehensive representations from extensive and heterogeneous data, FMs address the brittleness of

task-specific approaches. In other medical imaging domains, such as pathology20,21, chest radiography22–
28
     , and retinal imaging29, FMs trained on millions of images have demonstrated robust performance

across numerous clinical tasks with minimal annotation. This paradigm is particularly well suited for

MSK radiographs, where effective models must generalize across a wide range of conditions and

anatomical regions.
                                                                      S
                                                               ES
Despite their potential, MSK radiographs have yet to benefit from the foundation model approach.

                                                        PR
Current publicly available datasets remain too limited in size for effective pretraining because, no single

collection captures the full diversity of MSK disorders and anatomical regions. For example, the
                                                 IN
GRAZPEDWRI-DX dataset contains approximately 20,000 radiographs but focuses solely on fractures
                                         LE
and is confined to the wrist30. In practice, MSK radiographs encompass more than 100 conditions,
                                      C
including trauma-related fractures, degenerative joint diseases, endocrine disorders, malignancies, and
                                 TI
rare genetic syndromes, across 15 anatomical regions. Expanding beyond such narrow datasets toward
                              R
                           A
large, comprehensive collections is essential for developing comprehensive foundation models that can

advance MSK AI toward clinical translation.

Here, we present SKELEX (musculoSKELEtal X-ray foundation model), the first large-scale

foundation model for musculoskeletal radiographs, trained on the largest MSK X-ray dataset to date31

(Fig. 1a, b). Our dataset is uniquely comprehensive, comprising more than 1.2 million radiographs that

cover 15 body parts and over 89 musculoskeletal conditions. SKELEX was pretrained using a masked

autoencoding strategy and subsequently adapted to multiple diagnostic applications 32 (Fig. 1c).

SKELEX demonstrated strong performance across 12 musculoskeletal diagnostic tasks (Fig. 1d), even
                                      ARTICLE IN PRESS




in data-sparse settings, and also achieved unsupervised reconstruction-based anomaly localization by

generating abnormality maps without task-specific training (Fig. 1e). Building on this foundation, we

developed an interpretable model capable of predicting bone tumors (Fig. 1f), which we integrated into

a publicly accessible web application, demonstrating the translational potential of SKELEX in clinical

practice.

Construction of musculoskeletal radiograph foundation model (SKELEX)
                                                       PR
To learn rich visual representations of MSK radiographs, we developed SKELEX using a two-stage
                                               IN
self-supervised learning (SSL) pipeline. The model was initialized with a masked autoencoder (MAE)

                                       LE
pretrained on natural images (ImageNet-1K)32, which provided robust low-level representations. In the
                                    C
second stage, the pretrained backbone was further adapted to a large-scale collection of unlabeled MSK
                               TI
radiographs (1,296,540 images) from Seoul National University Hospital (SNUH-1M dataset), thereby
                           R
                        A
enabling domain-specific representation learning across diverse anatomical regions and disease states

(Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). The demographic diversity of SNUH-1M dataset is visualized in

Supplementary Figure 1.

During pretraining, SKELEX learned to reconstruct a wide range of musculoskeletal radiographs from

randomly masked inputs. This reconstruction ability generalized effectively to seven publicly available

MSK radiograph datasets (Supplementary Figure 2 and Supplementary Table 3). The model successfully

reconstructed images from various body parts, preserving key anatomical landmarks (Supplementary

Figures 3 and 4). It captured both normal anatomical features and pathologic findings. For instance,
                                       ARTICLE IN PRESS




when subtle radiographic clues of a bone tumor were visible, SKELEX successfully reconstructed the

lesion. However, when the tumor region was completely masked and no residual cues remained, the

model produced a normal-appearing reconstruction (Supplementary Figure 5). Similar behavior was

observed for other abnormalities, including fractures and osteoarthritis. These findings indicate that

SKELEX acquired detailed and clinically meaningful visual representations of MSK radiographs.

                                                              ES
To assess the utility of the learned representations, we evaluated SKELEX on 12 downstream diagnostic
                                                        PR
tasks using seven publicly available datasets, including fracture detection (FracAtlas33), bone tumor

                                                IN
classification (BTXRD34), osteoarthritis grading (OAI35), pes planus identification (PesPlanus dataset36),

and other musculoskeletal conditions30,31,37 (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Tables 4-15). Performance was
                                        LE

                                     C
                               TI

                            R

                         A

partitioning to prevent data leakage (details provided in the Methods).

Across the 12 tasks, SKELEX consistently demonstrated competitive or superior performance. To

isolate the impact of MSK domain-specific pretraining from architectural factors, we compared

SKELEX with its initialization model (ViT-MAE/I1K) under identical training protocols, achieving an
                                       ARTICLE IN PRESS




average relative performance improvement of 6.21%. SKELEX also consistently outperformed both

ImageNet- and medical-domain pretrained models. For example, in bone tumor detection, it achieved an

AUROC of 0.953, surpassing ImageNet-pretrained models (ViT-MAE/I1K: 0.884; ViT-L/I21K: 0.902;

ResNet-101: 0.903) and medical-domain models (BiomedCLIP: 0.914; Radio-DINO: 0.867).

Performance gains were most pronounced in fine-grained, anatomically specific tasks, where capturing

subtle structural nuances is critical. Specifically, SKELEX demonstrated relative performance increases

of 5.39% to 12.30% in bone tumor subtyping, 2.78% to 13.47% in pes planus identification, and 2.20%
                                                                     S
                                                              ES
to 7.66% in pediatric wrist fracture subtyping, compared to the best- and worst-performing baseline

                                                        PR
models. These results highlight the benefits of additional pretraining on large-scale musculoskeletal

radiographs beyond both general-purpose and broadly medical pretraining.

                            R

                         A

SKELEX achieved significant improvements in the shoulder (all p < 0.001) and elbow (all p < 0.05),

with additional gains in the humerus and hand compared to most baselines.

Label efficiency under limited supervision

To assess the data efficiency of the learned representations, we conducted label-efficiency analyses

using reduced training subsets (Fig. 2d–f). This is particularly important in musculoskeletal imaging,
                                       ARTICLE IN PRESS




where fine-grained annotations are scarce and many labels are limited to broad binary categories. For

example, in MURA31, the ‘abnormal’ label covers a highly heterogeneous spectrum of fractures, tumors,

and implants, requiring a model to learn robustly despite such information-sparse supervision.

Notably, SKELEX frequently outperformed baselines trained on full datasets using only half of the

training samples. In bone tumor detection, SKELEX achieved an AUROC of 0.941 using 50% of the

labeled training data, exceeding the best-performing baseline trained on the full dataset (BioMedCLIP:

0.914). In abnormality detection, SKELEX reached an AUROC of 0.855 with 50% of the data,
                                                                    S
                                                              ES
surpassing the strongest full-data baseline (BioMedCLIP: 0.846). These results support SKELEX as a

                                                       PR
data-efficient foundation model for musculoskeletal tasks.

Unsupervised reconstruction-based anomaly detection

A key advantage of SKELEX lies in its ability to localize abnormalities through the generation of

reconstruction-based error maps. Because the model is trained to reconstruct masked regions of
                                                                     S
radiographs, discrepancies between reconstructed and original images reveal areas that deviate from the
                                                               ES
model’s learned in-distribution anatomical patterns. This provides an unsupervised mechanism for
                                                        PR
abnormality localization without requiring explicit pixel-level annotations. To generate error maps, we

                                                IN
applied random masking to the input radiographs, reconstructed the masked regions using SKELEX,

and subtracted the reconstructed outputs from the original images. Ten random masks were used per
                                        LE
image to enhance stability, and the resulting pixel-wise squared errors were averaged to produce a final
                                     C
                                TI
mean squared error (MSE) map. This entirely unsupervised procedure required no additional training or

fine-tuning.
                            R
                         A
Evaluation across the BTXRD (bone tumor), FracAtlas (fracture), and OAI (knee osteoarthritis) datasets

demonstrated that the error maps localized a diverse range of pathologies (Fig. 3a–c). The MSE increase

was not limited to the primary lesion, but also captured secondary clinical signs, such as soft tissue

swelling, joint space narrowing, or the presence of orthopedic implants (Supplementary Figure 8a-c).

The mean reconstruction error was markedly elevated in abnormal cases relative to normal radiographs

(Supplementary Figure 8d-f). When the error map was compared to ground truth primary segmentation

mask, abnormal radiographs exhibited significantly higher Dice score compared to matched random
                                      ARTICLE IN PRESS




baseline masks (P < 0.0001, Fig. 3d–f), confirming the diagnostic relevance of the reconstruction error

signal. Without any additional supervision, SKELEX effectively achieved localization of skeletal

pathologies by leveraging reconstruction discrepancies.




Region-guided interpretable framework for multi-head bone tumor classification

Accurate interpretation of musculoskeletal radiographs requires identifying not only the disease type but
                                                                   S
also its anatomical context. Early and precise detection of primary bone tumors remains a major clinical
                                                             ES
challenge, as these lesions can mimic benign or post-traumatic findings on radiographs42. To enhance
                                                       PR
both diagnostic accuracy and interpretability, we extended SKELEX into a region-guided multi-head

                                               IN
bone tumor classification framework capable of simultaneously identifying anatomical regions and

multiple abnormality types, including bone tumors, fractures, and implants (Fig. 4a).
                                       LE
                                    C
The framework consisted of two sequential stages: (1) anatomical region identification, which localized
                               TI
key skeletal structures, and (2) region-specific inference, where each localized region was independently
                           R
                        A
analyzed for multiple abnormalities. The model was fine-tuned using three independent datasets,

SNUH-BoneTumor, BTXRD34-Center 1 (three hospitals in China), and FracAtlas33, to ensure broad

anatomical and pathological diversity (Supplementary Table 23). To effectively capture diverse

information across heterogeneous datasets, we implemented a label-masking strategy that computed loss

only for available labels, as public datasets frequently lacked complementary annotations (for example,

fracture labels in BTXRD dataset were masked).

The multi-head local classifier demonstrated strong performance in 5-fold cross-validation across all

prediction tasks (Fig. 4b, c). It accurately identified anatomical regions (average AUROC = 0.999) and
                                       ARTICLE IN PRESS




achieved AUROC values exceeding 0.9 for all abnormality classifications, including bone tumor,

fracture, and implant detection. Given the near-perfect discrimination observed in certain tasks

(AUROC ≥ 0.99), the numbers of positive and negative samples for all tasks in this analysis are

provided for transparency (Supplementary Table 24). On the internal test set, the model exhibited strong

performance across all bone tumor and fracture categories, accurately distinguishing between benign

and malignant tumors as well as pathological and non-pathological fractures (Fig. 4d, e). The complete

region-guided framework also maintained robust performance on independent external datasets,
                                                                     S
BTXRD-Center 2 and BTXRD-Center 3, sourced from Radiopaedia and MedPix, respectively (Fig. 4f).
                                                              ES
                                                        PR
To evaluate the sensitivity of the multi-head bone tumor classifier to the precision of the region-proposal

accuracy, we conducted controlled perturbation experiments systematically modifying the ground-truth
                                                IN
bounding boxes by size and center shifts (Supplementary Figure 9). The model achieved optimal
                                        LE
performance with the unshifted boxes and was highly tolerant to small-to-moderate localization errors.
                                     C
In contrast, larger perturbations led to marked AUROC declines. Severe box shrinking or large center
                               TI
shifts sharply degraded performance because the lesion was partially or entirely excluded from the crop.
                             R
                         A
Large box expansions introduced more non-informative background. However, because the lesion

typically remained within the field of view, performance was comparatively less affected. These results

suggest that the classifier benefits from accurate region proposals, yet remains robust to modest

localization inaccuracies.

We provide qualitative case-based evidence of the model’s potential to identify clinically meaningful

findings that extended beyond the labels provided in publicly available datasets. In BTXRD, which

lacks explicit fracture annotations despite containing bone tumor cases with fractures, the model

accurately identified both bone tumors and fractures, thereby enriching the information represented by
                                       ARTICLE IN PRESS




the official dataset labels and aligning with clinical reports (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Figure 10a). In

MURA, which offers only a binary abnormality label without specifying the type of abnormality

(fracture, tumor, or implant type), the model successfully inferred detailed findings, including bone

tumors, fractures, implants, and specific anatomical locations (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Figure 10b).

Region-guided interpretable framework model predictions that were absent from the original dataset

labels showed strong concordance with clinical notes documented by a board-certified orthopedic

surgeon. To demonstrate potential clinical applicability, we deployed the model as a publicly accessible
                                                                     S
web-based diagnostic interface that allows users to upload radiographs and automatically estimate the
                                                               ES
probabilities of bone tumors and fractures (Fig. 6). This prototype serves as a proof of concept for real-
                                                        PR
world musculoskeletal diagnosis, demonstrating the translational potential of SKELEX-derived models.

Discussion

This study introduces SKELEX, a large-scale vision foundation model for MSK radiographs, trained on

more than 1.2 million unlabeled images using a masked autoencoding strategy. SKELEX learns

radiograph-specific representations directly from large-scale, uncurated clinical data, enabling the model

to capture broad anatomical variation and diverse disease manifestations while minimizing the need for

extensive expert annotation. The model’s strong adaptability across multiple diagnostic tasks supports

the notion that domain-specific self-supervised pretraining can be more effective than transferring from
                                                                    S
models trained on natural images.                             ES
                                                        PR
Earlier musculoskeletal AI models were typically developed for a single disease category or a limited

anatomical region, and many relied on small, curated datasets. Consequently, these models often lacked
                                                  IN
robustness when applied across different clinical contexts. In contrast, SKELEX was trained on data
                                        LE
encompassing diverse anatomical regions and conditions, enabling it to perform consistently across
                                     C
                               TI
twelve downstream diagnostic tasks and to reconstruct masked radiographs from multiple external

                            R
datasets. This broad representational capacity is significant because musculoskeletal radiographs
                        A
frequently exhibit multiple coexisting findings, such as degenerative changes, fractures, implants, and

soft-tissue abnormalities. These features influence one another both visually and clinically, and accurate

interpretation often depends on integrating them rather than evaluating each in isolation. Models that

fail to account for this complexity may misclassify or overlook relevant findings, whereas SKELEX,

through its pretraining on diverse anatomical and pathological patterns, offers a more advantageous

foundation for jointly modeling these features.

A key finding of this study is the label efficiency demonstrated by SKELEX. Expert annotation in
                                       ARTICLE IN PRESS




musculoskeletal imaging is time-intensive, and rare conditions are often underrepresented in existing

datasets. SKELEX achieved strong performance using substantially fewer labeled examples, suggesting

that large-scale self-supervised pretraining can meaningfully reduce the annotation burden required to

develop clinically useful models, particularly for rare diseases where assembling large, well-annotated

datasets is challenging. Furthermore, SKELEX exhibited unsupervised reconstruction-based pathology

localization by generating reconstruction-based error maps that highlighted potentially pathological

regions without additional supervision. This offers a form of interpretable visual attribution without

task-specific training.
                                                                     S
                                                               ES
                                                        PR
Using the pretrained model, we further developed an interpretable region-guided bone tumor detection

system. By first localizing anatomically meaningful regions and then performing region-specific multi-
                                                IN
task inference, the system demonstrated robustness across independent external datasets and
                                         LE
demonstrated the potential to enrich public datasets that contained only binary abnormality labels by
                                     C
providing more detailed distinctions among tumors, fractures, and implants. However, it is important to
                                TI
explicitly delineate that our external validation was done specifically on the bone tumor application, not
                            R
                          A
for all downstream tasks. Our findings show the translational potential of SKELEX in bone tumor

applications. Additionally, a web-based interface was developed to illustrate how the model could

potentially be used in real-world clinical and research environments.

This work carries implications for the broader goal of democratizing medical AI. By releasing SKELEX

as a pretrained model, institutions with limited computational or annotation resources can adapt it to

their own clinical environments using comparatively small labeled datasets. Its strong label efficiency

also creates new opportunities for applying AI to rare musculoskeletal diseases, where large, well-

annotated cohorts are difficult to obtain. Together, these features may help reduce disparities in access to
                                        ARTICLE IN PRESS




high-quality musculoskeletal diagnostic tools across regions and healthcare systems.

Our benchmarking against contemporary medical foundation models further clarifies when MSK-

specific pretraining is most beneficial. BiomedCLIP performed competitively for fracture detection,

likely reflecting exposure to common fracture radiographs and descriptions during large-scale image-

text pretraining. In contrast, SKELEX demonstrated its clearest advantages in bone tumor detection, a

long-tail clinical problem in which cases are rare and therefore less consistently represented in general

biomedical corpora. This divergence suggests that MSK-specific pretraining is particularly valuable for
                                                                      S
                                                               ES
rare or specialized MSK conditions where generic medical models may have limited exposure or weaker

                                                         PR
domain priors. Together, these results emphasize the clinical usefulness of SKELEX's transferability and

label efficiency in settings where expert annotation is scarce. More broadly, these findings motivate
                                                 IN
future scalable medical AI efforts to incorporate diverse MSK radiographs during pretraining. This will
                                         LE
enable robust, MSK-focused systems and strengthen general medical vision models, which must
                                     C
perform reliably across heterogeneous anatomy and disease prevalence.
                                TI
                             R
Several limitations should be acknowledged. SKELEX was trained primarily on data from a single
                         A
institution, which may constrain geographic and demographic diversity. We intentionally restricted

training to this dataset and did not incorporate publicly available datasets to avoid any possibility of data

leakage during external evaluation. Nonetheless, the model successfully reconstructed masked

radiographs from seven external datasets and performed robustly across diverse diagnostic tasks,

supporting its broad applicability. While SKELEX demonstrated strong adaptability and label efficiency

across 12 diverse downstream tasks, these broad evaluations were primarily conducted on held-out test

splits from their respective public datasets. Currently, our strongest evidence for external

generalizability is concentrated specifically on the bone tumor application. Future work must extend this
                                       ARTICLE IN PRESS




rigorous independent external validation framework to the other downstream clinical tasks to fully

confirm their real-world generalizability. Second, the current model is based on the ViT-L architecture,

which necessitates input downsampling to 224 × 224 pixels. This constraint may theoretically reduce

sensitivity to subtle abnormalities, such as tiny non-displaced fracture or small bone tumor with faint

medullary lucency. While our resolution ablation study indicated that classification performance for

bone tumors remained stable from 224 × 224 down to 112 × 112, performance drop occurred below

this threshold (Supplementary Figure 11). We recognize that diagnostic tasks requiring high-resolution
                                                                     S
information may benefit from architectures optimized for high resolution feature learning. Third,
                                                               ES
although this study shows that large-scale self-supervised pretraining on MSK radiographs improves
                                                        PR
downstream performance, our experiments were focused on a MAE architecture and did not include
                                                IN
direct comparisons with other self-supervised framework or image-text multimodal pretraining. Our

                                        LE
primary aim was to demonstrate that scaling self-supervised pretraining on diverse MSK radiographs

                                     C
yields transferable representation that improve clinically relevant tasks. While MAE provides an
                                TI
effective and scalable objective, future works should systemically benchmark alternative self-supervised
                            R
                         A
paradigms and multimodal image-text pretraining under matched condition, particularly when high-

quality paired reports or curated captions are available. Finally, this study focused exclusively on

radiographs. Future research should explore multimodal approaches that integrate imaging data with

clinical metadata, laboratory results, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT)

findings, and longitudinal outcomes to enable more comprehensive diagnostic and prognostic reasoning.

In conclusion, SKELEX demonstrates that large-scale, self-supervised learning of musculoskeletal

radiographs can yield a powerful and adaptable foundation model. By leveraging abundant unlabeled
                                       ARTICLE IN PRESS




data, SKELEX provides a scalable framework for musculoskeletal imaging applications and presents a

practical path toward more consistent, accurate, and accessible diagnostic support in clinical practice.

Methods

Large-scale visual pretraining of SKELEX

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB

No. H-2203-079-1306). All radiographs and associated metadata were retrospectively collected and de-

identified prior to analysis. Because this study utilized archival imaging data without direct patient

contact, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

                                                                      S
To pretrain SKELEX, we assembled a large-scale musculoskeletal radiograph dataset (SNUH-1M) for
                                                               ES
self-supervised learning. SNUH-1M comprised of 1,296,540 unlabeled musculoskeletal radiographs
                                                         PR
retrieved from the hospital’s PACS between 2010 and 2016. These radiographs covered 15 anatomical

                                                 IN
regions and included a broad range of traumatic, neoplastic, degenerative, inflammatory, and

postoperative conditions, thereby reflecting real-world clinical diversity (Supplementary Tables 1, 2).
                                         LE
                                     C
SKELEX was pretrained using a masked autoencoder strategy32. We selected the MAE approach
                                TI
because masked image modeling has been shown to capture fine-grained, patch-level information
                            R
                         A
effectively, which is particularly important for radiographs that often contain small, subtle lesions43.

Each radiograph was resized to 224×224 pixels and divided into patches of size 16×16, a random subset

of which was masked, and the model was trained to reconstruct the missing patches from the visible

context. The reconstruction loss was defined as the mean squared error (MSE) between the predicted

and true pixels only on masked patches, consistent with prior MAE works. This reconstruction objective

encouraged the model to learn contextual and structural priors in musculoskeletal radiographs, including

cortical continuity, trabecular pattern, joint alignment, and soft-tissue contours (Supplementary Figures

1 and 2).
                                       ARTICLE IN PRESS




Pretraining followed established masked autoencoder frameworks32, with modifications tailored to

radiographic data (Supplementary Table 17). The encoder consisted of 24 blocks with embedding size

1024, and the decoder consisted of 8 blocks with embedding size 512. The hidden activation was

GELU. The encoder was initialized with ViT-MAE ImageNet-1K pretrained weights from the Hugging

Face Transformers library, using a ViT-Large backbone. Because the ImageNet-pretrained architecture

expects three-channel (RGB) input, single-channel grayscale radiographs were replicated across three

channels to match the required input dimensionality.
                                                                     S
                                                              ES
Optimization was performed with the AdamW optimizer (1 = 0.9, 2 = 0.999) with a weight decay of

                                                        PR
0.05. The learning rate was set to 7.5×10-5 with a cosine decay schedule and a warmup ratio of 0.05. The

batch size was 128, and pretraining was conducted for 50 epochs. Normalized pixel loss was disabled to
                                                IN
avoid distortion from the large, uniform black background regions inherent to radiographs
                                        LE
(Supplementary Figure 12). A masking ratio of 75% was applied. Data augmentations included random
                                     C
resized cropping (20-100% of the image area) and horizontal flipping. No manual labels or anatomical
                                TI
supervision were used during pretraining. Pretraining was conducted on single NVIDIA RTX A6000
                            R
                         A
Ada (40 GB) GPU with a compute budget of 1630 GPU hours (wall-clock).

Label efficiency

To evaluate label efficiency, we assessed how model performance varied as the number of labeled

training examples was reduced while maintaining a fixed independent test set. For each task, 10% of the
                                         ARTICLE IN PRESS




full dataset was first randomly held out to construct an independent test set, which remained unchanged

across all label-fraction experiments. The remaining 90% of the data constituted the training pool.

From this training pool, we randomly sampled 10%, 20%, 50%, 90%, or 100% of the available labeled

data to simulate varying levels of label availability. Sampling was stratified to preserve class balance

within each subset. For each subset size, SKELEX was fine-tuned using the same optimization settings

as in the full-data experiments, and the checkpoint yielding the highest validation AUROC was selected.

Final performance was then evaluated on the same fixed independent test set for all label fractions.

                                                   IN

Leakage Mitigation and Duplicate Detection
                             R
                          A
To mitigate potential data leakage, data splitting was performed according to the availability of patient

identifiers. For datasets with patient identifiers, strict patient-level splitting was applied, assigning all

images from a given patient exclusively to a single partition (training, validation, or test).

For datasets without patient identifiers, image-level splitting was used. To reduce structural redundancy

between training and evaluation splits, duplicate detection was performed across partitions. Exact

duplicates were identified using file-level MD5 hashing and removed. Structurally similar images were

assessed using the Structural Similarity Index (SSIM). To focus on plausible duplicate candidates and

limit unnecessary comparisons, SSIM was computed only between cross-partition image pairs sharing
                                      ARTICLE IN PRESS




identical metadata: for BTXRD, matching was based on age and sex; for FracAtlas, only image pairs

from the same anatomical region (e.g., leg, hand) were compared. Image pairs with SSIM ≥ 0.95 were


considered near-duplicates, and one image from each cross-partition pair was removed from the

evaluation split.

Sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate robustness to near-duplicate exclusion. For each SSIM

threshold (0.90, 0.95, 0.98) and without SSIM-based exclusion, cross-partition image pairs exceeding
                                                                   S
the threshold were removed from the evaluation set. Model performance was assessed on the held-out
                                                             ES
test set using a 5,000-iteration bootstrap, where AUROC was first averaged across the 5 cross-validation
                                                      PR
folds for each bootstrap sample. The mean AUROC and 95% confidence intervals were derived from

                                               IN
the bootstrap distribution and compared across SSIM thresholds.

counterpart without any task-specific fine-tuning. The residual map should be interpreted as a measure

Interpretable, region guided multi-headed bone tumor classification
                                        ARTICLE IN PRESS




We developed a two-stage framework to enable interpretable detection of bone tumors, fractures, and

orthopedic implants. The first stage localized anatomically meaningful regions, and the second stage

performed multi-headed classification within each region. Anatomical regions were identified using a

modern real-time object detector (YOLO11x)44. The detector was trained on 4,049 radiographs from our

institutional dataset, annotated with bounding boxes for 29 anatomical regions and auxiliary abnormal

bone regions. The bounding box annotations adhered to AO Foundation’s communication system for

defining anatomical locations45. For regional classification, SKELEX was fine-tuned using a multi-
                                                                        S
headed architecture consisted of five clinically relevant output heads: (1) global abnormality detection
                                                                ES
(binary); (2) bone tumor subtype classification (four classes: malignant, intermediate, benign, normal);
                                                         PR
(3) anatomical location classification (29 classes); (4) fracture category classification (three classes:
                                                   IN
neoplastic pathologic fracture, non-neoplastic fracture, normal); (5) orthopedic implant presence

                                         LE
detection (binary). We adopt a multi-head classification design, implemented as a single shared output

                                      C
tensor partitioned into task-specific groups. Supervised fine-tuning was performed using a masked
                                TI
multi-task objective to accommodate datasets with incomplete label coverage. For each image and label
                             R
𝑐, a binary label (𝑦𝑐 ) and a mask (𝑚𝑐 ) were defined, where (𝑚𝑐 = 1) indicated that the label was
                         A
unknown or not applicable. Loss and evaluation terms were computed only for labels where (𝑚𝑐 = 0).

optimized using masked categorical cross-entropy (CE). The total loss was calculated as the equally

Evaluation and statistical analysis

For model comparison, statistical significance was evaluated using a non-parametric paired bootstrap

(5,000 iterations). The unit of resampling was defined according to the data-splitting strategy: for
                                                                     S
datasets with patient identifiers, patients in the held-out test set were resampled with replacement and all
                                                               ES
corresponding images were included; for datasets without patient identifiers, individual test images were
                                                        PR
resampled with replacement. This approach preserved both the original sample size and the paired

                                                IN
relationship between models evaluated on identical data.

                                         LE

                                      C
