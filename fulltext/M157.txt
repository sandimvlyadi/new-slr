Abstract
Foundation models (FM) offer a promising alternative to supervised deep learning (DL) by enabling greater flexibility and
generalizability without relying on large, labeled datasets. This study investigates the performance of supervised DL models
and pre-trained FM embeddings in classifying radiographic features related to knee osteoarthritis. We analyzed 44,985 knee
radiographs from the Osteoarthritis Initiative dataset. Two convolutional neural network models (ResNet18 and ConvNeXt-
Small) were trained to classify osteophytes, joint space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis, and Kellgren-Lawrence grades
(KLG). These models were compared against two FM: BiomedCLIP, a multimodal vision-language model pre-trained on
diverse medical images and text, and RAD-DINO vision transformer model pre-trained exclusively on chest radiographs.
We extracted image embeddings from both FMs and used XGBoost classifiers to perform downstream classification. Perfor-
mance was assessed using a comprehensive classification metrics appropriate for binary and multi-class classification tasks.
DL models outperformed FM-based approaches across all tasks. ConvNeXt achieved the highest performance in predicting
KLG, with a weighted Cohen’s kappa of 0.880 and higher AUC in binary tasks. BiomedCLIP and RAD-DINO performed
similarly, and BiomedCLIP's prior exposure to knee radiographs during pretraining led to only slight improvements. Zero-
shot classification using BiomedCLIP correctly identified 91.14% of knee radiographs, with most failures associated with low
image quality. Grad-CAM visualizations revealed DL models, particularly ConvNeXt, reliably focused on clinically relevant
regions. While FMs offer promising utility in auxiliary imaging tasks, supervised DL remains superior for fine-grained
radiographic feature classification in domains with limited pretraining representation, such as musculoskeletal imaging.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

In this retrospective study, we utilized knee radiographs
from the Osteoarthritis Initiative (OAI), a 10-year pro-
spective observational study of osteoarthritis sponsored
by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) [25]. The ini-
tial dataset comprised 55,179 radiographic readings cor-
responding to 24,546 bilateral knee images from 4796
patients. Following OAI guidelines, duplicate readings
were removed by keeping only the most recent evaluation
for each image, followed by excluding the exams with-
out Kellgren-Lawrence grade (KLG) reports resulting in
45,486 unique readings. Of these, 45,092 images (from
24,058 bilateral knee examinations for 4508 patients) were
available through the AWS repository as of February 28,
2025 (Fig. 1).


Image Preprocessing

We processed all knee radiographs using our in-house
pipeline to ensure consistent and high-quality images for
analysis. The original DICOM files were first converted to
PNG format using a 2nd to 98th percentile intensity scal-
ing approach. This method was essential because many OAI
DICOM files lacked reliable metadata for lookup tables,
window width, and window center, leading to inconsisten-
cies in image rendering. Standard DICOM conversion tech-
niques, such as VOI or modality LUT transformations and
fixed window/level settings, resulted in substantial variation
in brightness and contrast, rendering the images unsuitable
for consistent analysis. Supplementary Fig. 1 shows a sam-
ple of different-approach results on a single image. The per-
centile scaling method offered a robust solution by mapping
the intensity values between the 2nd and 98th percentiles
of each image to the full dynamic range of the PNG for-
mat. This approach effectively standardized image appear-
ance across the dataset regardless of the original acquisition
parameters or metadata inconsistencies.
   After conversion, we applied a validated knee segmenta-
tion algorithm (DUKE model) to automatically crop each
image to focus on the knee joint region [21]. This cropping
step successfully processed 44,985 unilateral knee images,
removing irrelevant background elements and standardizing
the anatomical region of interest. As a final enhancement,
we applied contrast limited adaptive histogram equalization
(CLAHE) to improve the visibility of subtle joint space and
bone margin features critical for osteoarthritis assessment,
while also implementing random horizontal flipping during
training for data augmentation.                                  Fig. 1  Patient selection flowchart
