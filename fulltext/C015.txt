1 Introduction
Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) severity assessment from radiographs remains an important task for treatment planning and disease
monitoring. However, automated Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) grading of radiographs provides a repeatable alternative to manual
assessment, but there are challenges with reliable discrimination between adjacent severity grades. The deep learning-based
radiographic analysis has shown significant potential for assessing KOA (1) . A Systematic Review and Meta analysis revealed that
advanced KL grades showed better diagnostic performance than early grades and this was consistently reported to be difficult
around subtle structural changes (2) . Reader-level agreement has also been shown to be good for external-dataset evaluation,
supporting automated grading, as well as sensitivity to dataset variation (3) . Hence, the impact of clinically meaningful KOA
classification is not limited to simply the amount of correct classifications, but must also consider ordinal consistency, class-
specific behavior, generalization and computational feasibility.
   One of the most important strategies for radiographic KOA analysis is transfer learning, which can help to reduce training
requirements and maintain discriminative image features. The results of the KOA classification are encouraging with transfer
based feature engineering, deep neural networks, advanced detection architectures and pretrained convolutional models (4–8) .
Kinger (9) proved the feasibility of deep architectures for automatic severity grading and Rani et al. (10) presented the severity
grading of radiographic KOA using CNN. Together, these results support the use of convolutional feature representations, yet
the typical categorical training uses KL grades as unordered classes due to the ordered clinical progression from Grade 0 to
Grade 4 across the grades.
   In recent years, attention mechanisms, multi-scale processing, ensembles and deeper feature integration have been among
the research interests in architecture. Representation learning over multiple scales has achieved higher representation quality
for severity-sensitive tasks (11) and multi-network ensembles have shown robust classification performance on OAI-based
radiographs (12) . Further examples of the usefulness of advanced feature extraction techniques are found in inception-
based transfer learning (13) , in comparative deep architectures (14) and in multi-scale attention frameworks (15) . Multi-network
ensembles, transformer branches, multi-resolution processing and stacked attention modules, however, increment the
complexity of the model and computational demands. Thus there is a practical need for a single-backbone architecture that
can maintain severity ordering without relying on multiple independently trained networks.
   The use of ordinal learning as the base for KL grading is appropriate since adjacent grade errors are of different
clinical meaning than large grade displacement errors. Ordinal loss formulations have shown the benefits of severity-driven
optimization (16) and automated DNN grading has also shown the feasibility of structured radiographic classification (17) . Stable
deep representation optimization by residual learning (18) and evidence of severity-related structural information by OAI-
based radiographic predictor modelling (19) justify deep representation. Longitudinal gait-based prediction studies are also
indicative of more general interest in structured KOA severity estimation (20) . In recent years, the research of KOA grading
has been extended to the field of transfer learning, deep network optimization, multimodal transformer and traditional-deep
feature fusion (21–26) . However, there is still a practical research gap in the use of a computationally compact single-backbone
radiographic grading with explicit supervision to determine exact class identity, cumulative severity boundaries and grade-
distance error. To fill this gap, SOBR-KneeNet proposes a shared ConvNeXt-Tiny encoder with categorical, cumulative-ordinal
and residual-grade supervision. The main contribution is the severity-ordered representation learning in the optimization
without the need for fusion of multiple transformer models, multiple model inference, or iterative post-processing; and the
evaluation using accuracy, balanced accuracy, macro F1 score, grade mean absolute error, quadratic weighted kappa and within-
one-grade accuracy.

2 Methodology
SOBR-KneeNet develops established methodological principles into a single severity-aware training framework rather than
modifying a single predecessor architecture. Transfer-learning based KOA classification serves as the foundation for pretrained
classification of radiographic features (4,9) and ordinal severity modelling follows the principle of explicitly penalizing grade
displacement instead of assuming a uniform penalty for each class error (16) . In addition to stable representation optimization
and continuous grade-error modelling, another basis of residual learning is provided (18) . Attention-based, multi-scale
and ensemble architectures (11,12,15) provide important performance benchmarks, but impose extra branches, resolutions,
attention modules and/or independently optimized networks. Instead, SOBR-KneeNet simultaneously shares a ConvNeXt-Tiny
representation to the categorical goal, cumulative-boundary goal and residual-grade goal.

2.1 Dataset and severity labels
The knee osteoarthritis severity dataset consists of 8,260 fixed-flexion knee radiographs from the Osteoarthritis Initiative (OAI)
that are publicly available. Predefined partitions: There are 5,778 training images, 826 validation images and 1,656 test images.

2.2 Radiograph preprocessing and augmentation
All the X-rays are processed through grayscale-preserving contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization (CLAHE), resized
to 256 × 256 pixels and then normalized with pretrained-backbone statistics. CLAHE reduces intensity saturation all over
the image while enhancing the contrast in the local regions showing the boundaries between the joint spaces. Training
augmentation consists of horizontal reflection and limited geometric or photo-metrical perturbation. The validation and test
transformations are still deterministic, except for horizontal-flip test-time augmentation for final inference. Effective-number
weighting mitigates the problem of class imbalance without creating a static synthetic data set of 10K per grade.

2.3 SOBR-KneeNet architecture
The proposed architecture is shown in Figure 1. A CLAHE enhanced radiograph is fed into a ConvNeXt-Tiny model that was
pretrained on ImageNet-22K and fine-tuned on ImageNet-1K. The encoder is composed of a convolutional stem and four
hierarchical stages, each of which features depthwise convolution, layer normalization, pointwise expansion, GELU activation
and pointwise projection. Global average pooling reduces the last feature map to a shared embedding. The shared embedding is
communicated to three lightweight output heads: a five-logit categorical head, a four-logit cumulative ordinal head and a scalar
residual-grade head. It has 28,628,106 trainable parameters.

2.6 Experimental settings and hyperparameter conﬁguration
All experiments were run on an NVIDIA CUDA enabled GPU using PyTorch 2.12.1 and CUDA 12.6, CUdNN 9.1.0. Compared
to the standard single precision, automatic mixed precision decreased memory traffic. AdamW set the learning rate for the
backbone and the head separately and decayed them with cosine decay. The backbone was frozen for three epochs and then
was progressively fine-tuned. Validation macro F1-score was used in early stopping (patience 15). The best checkpoint was at
epoch 33 and training ended at epoch 48. Full reproducible configuration is given in Table 2.

2.7 Evaluation measures
Accuracy indicates correctness in terms of exact grade. Balanced accuracy is the average of the recall scores at five grades. Macro
F1-score is equal to every grade’s F1-score and weighted F1-score considers test support. The mean absolute error is an average
of the ordinal distance. Quadratic weighted kappa gives more penalty to opposing views that are further apart. The proportion
satisfying | − 𝑦| ≤ 1 is called the within one grade accuracy. Combined reporting ensures that there is no reliance on a single
measure that is sensitive to imbalance.

3 Results and Discussion

SOBR-KneeNet was able to make 1141 out of the 1656 test radiographs correct, giving an accuracy rate of 68.90%. The accuracy
of the balanced accuracy was 71.20%, the macro F1-score was 70.38% and the quadratic weighted kappa was 0.8450. The results
of Grade MAE: 0.3904 and within-one-grade accuracy: 95.59% indicate a good level of severity consistency. Class-wise values
and aggregate values are given in Table 3. The best recall is obtained in the grade 4 (90.20%) and the highest F1-score is obtained

Grade 1 (36.85%) classes, which indicate that it is the hardest to distinguish from these two classes visually.

3.3 Training behavior
The loss on the training data and the macro F1 score on the validation data are plotted in Figure 6 for 48 epochs completed.
Training loss declined from 1.4356 to 0.6503. The F1 score obtained by the validation macro increased sharply in the first 12
epochs and peaked at the checkpoint value of 0.6928 at epoch 33. Subsequent validation fluctuations remained small while
training loss continued to decrease, indicating stable convergence after the peak validation checkpoint. To avoid selecting sub-
optimal later checkpoints, early stopping was used, which kept the weights at epoch 33. In the recorded environment, the
backbone unfreezing caused mean value of the epoch duration to stay around 13.3 seconds.

3.4 Comparative study
Calculations were performed to determine the difference between SOBR-KneeNet and existing models that have been evaluated
on the same 8,260 image OAI derived dataset and fixed 1,656 image test split as shown in Table 4. The accuracy of SOBR-KneeNet
exceeded ResNet-50 V2 by 3.54 percentage points, DenseNet-121 by 2.59 percentage points and ResNet34-ViT hybrid by 2.11
percentage points. Macro F1-score improved by 1.71 points from the ResNet34-ViT class average reported F1-scores. Grade-4
recall increased from 88.24% to 90.20%. The optimized 8-model ensemble reported by Pi et al. (12) achieved the accuracy of
76.93% and F1-score of 76.65%, outperforming the accuracy of the single model SOBR-KneeNet. Pi et al. need eight optimized
independently backbones and several input resolutions. The computational design of SOBR-KneeNet is extremely simple with
only 28.63 million-parameter backbone and 256-pixel input stream. A direct comparison of runtimes is not yet suitable, because
of the variation in hardware, cross validation and model selection methods.

3.5 Discussion
SOBR-KneeNet achieved 68.90% exact-grade accuracy, 71.20% balanced accuracy, 70.38% macro F1-score, 0.3904 grade
MAE, 0.8450 quadratic weighted kappa and 95.59% within-one-grade accuracy. The combination of categorical supervision,
cumulative severity boundaries, residual-grade estimation and monotonicity regularization provides a plausible explanation
for the strong ordinal consistency. Under the fixed comparison setting, DenseNet-121 and ResNet-50 V2 achieved accuracy
improvements of 2.59 and 2.11 percentage points, respectively, when compared with ResNet34-ViT and an improvement of
3.54 percentage points. These gains are for severity-aware optimization, rather than inference-time fusion. For SOBR-KneeNet,
Pi et al. (12) reported higher accuracy of 76.93% with an optimized eight network ensemble, which is an important accuracy-
complexity trade-off rather than the absolute state-of-the-art superiority. The single backbone design with 28.63 million
parameters is then a computationally simpler model for the ordered five-class grading.
   The main constraint is still Grade 1 (F1-score 36.85%). This is consistent with the overall literature not an isolated failure.
Pooled sensitivities have been reported as 64.00% for KL Grade 1 and 90.32% for KL Grade 4, making the early detection
of the subtle radiographic changes significantly more challenging (2) . Huang et al. (23) also found that Grade 1 is the most
challenging class in an externally validated multimodal system with significantly more complex models and clinical covariates.
The SOBR-KneeNet confusion pattern then still shows the same pattern of errors around the same grade levels, resulting in
clinically consistent difficulty as expected for KL grades. Avoid claims of superior early stage detection, better boundary-focused
sampling, better localized joint space representation and higher resolution processing of regions of interest are more appropriate
path forward.
   In recent literature, much higher reported performance under different experimental conditions is found. Bukhari and
Altaf (22) achieved 97% accuracy using transfer learning with EfficientNetB3 and Yilmaz and Solak (25) achieved 85.39% accuracy
using both traditional and deep features with joint fusion. By combining radiographs, clinical covariates, cross-modal attention
and uncertainty-aware gating, Huang et al. (23) reached a balanced accuracy of 87.6% and a QWK of 0.900 on an independent,
external cohort. It is inappropriate to compare the results of such studies directly, since the evaluation partitions, modalities,
preprocessing techniques, validation procedures and computational requirements are all different. SOBR-KneeNet therefore
contributes primarily through compact severity-structured optimization: one 256 × 256 input stream, one ConvNeXt-Tiny
backbone, lightweight auxiliary heads during training and calibrated categorical inference. Some practical use can be screening
support with radiographs, severity pre-grading, assisting second readers and prioritizing cases for specialist review. To widely
use this in clinical practice, independent multicenter validation, prospective clinical evaluation, probability-calibration analysis
and targeted improvement of the discrimination for Grade 1 are required.

4 Conclusion
SOBR-KneeNet addresses the objective of computationally efficient, severity-ordered five-class knee osteoarthritis grading
from radiographs. The main novelty is in the severity-aware optimization by a single ConvNeXt-Tiny backbone shared by
the categorical, cumulative-boundary and residual-grade objectives. The auxiliary severity branches are not used as multi-
model ensembles or transformer-fusion architectures: They are lightweight training regularizers and the final inference is done
using calibrated categorical probabilities. This design is able to maintain explicit KL order information without bringing the
complexity of multi-network inference.
   The experimental evaluation results are 68.90% accuracy, 71.20% balanced accuracy, 70.38% macro F1 score, 0.3904 grade
MAE, 0.8450 quadratic weighted kappa and 95.59% within-one-grade accuracy. The reported comparison setting shows the
benefit of severity-structured training with reported accuracy improvements of 2.11%, 2.59% and 3.54% over the selected

baseline networks ResNet34-ViT, DenseNet-121 and ResNet-50 V2. The results showed that grade 4 recall was 90.20% while
grade 1 was the most restricting with a F1 score of 36.85%.
   Practical applications may involve automated pre-grading of radiographs, second reader support and prioritizing worklists
and making decisions based on the severity of the images in clinical settings without a large number of specialists. SOBR-
KneeNet should be used as an assistive grading system and not as a stand-alone system for diagnosis. Independent multicenter
validation, higher resolution joint space regions, boundary-focused sampling Grade 1, assessment of calibration and prospective
clinical workflow evaluation should be the focus of future research. Such extensions can provide generalizability, reliability,
clinical usefulness beyond the current fixed radiographic benchmark.
