Ordinal classification for knee osteoarthritis x‑rays using

Abstract
Knee Osteoarthritis is a progressive and chronic knee joint disease that shows symptoms of
pain, stiffness, and swelling and is diagnosed by knee radiographs. These radiographs are
evaluated by radiologists using Kellgren and Lawrence’s (KL) grading scheme. Accord-
ing to this scheme, the knee radiograph is graded from KL grade 0 (i.e., Normal) to KL
grade 4 (i.e., Severe) to assess the KOA severity. For an automated system, the first step is
to localize knee joints from knee radiographs and then classify them according to the KL
grading system. In this work, we have proposed vision-based transformers to localize knee
joints. We also proposed a single-stage model to localize the knee and classify KOA using
a single model. We also explored and compared the performance of various transformers
using a multiclass image classification strategy. Since KL grades maintain intrinsic ordinal
information of KL grades, we employed an ordinal classification-based strategy to improve
automated knee osteoarthritis classification performance. A total of 10 models are trained
and validated on multiple datasets, and visualizations are generated for the best model for
better comprehension. Our model obtained state-of-the-art results for knee detection and
ordinal X-ray classification for KOA. Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) tests also indicate
that the ordinal classification approach significantly improves the performance of models
over traditional image classification approaches.

Ordinal classification · Vision transformer

1 Introduction

Knee Osteoarthritis (KOA) is the typical type of Osteoarthritis (OA) affecting millions
of people over the age of 50. Still, it can also occur in younger individuals, especially
those who have suffered a knee injury or have a family history of the condition. It occurs
when the cartilage in the knee joint wears down over time, leading to pain, stiffness,
and swelling. This can make it difficult to perform everyday activities, such as walking,
climbing stairs, and bending down [1]. Several treatment options are available for knee
osteoarthritis, ranging from lifestyle modifications to medication to surgery, depending
on the severity of the condition.
    For diagnosis patients’ clinical, blood, and radiographic assessments are done. Radi-
ologists use radiographs to observe various signs of KOA indications, including joint
space narrowing, bony lump formations along the edges (osteophytes), and sclerosis [2,
3].
    In 2014, the first international workshop on early OA was held in Kyoto, refining the
criteria for identifying early OA. According to these criteria [4, 5], three conditions must
be met to classify a case as early OA. First, a tool score, such as the Knee Injury and Osteo-
arthritis Outcome Score (KOOS), should be less than or equal to 85% in at least two of the
following categories: pain, symptoms, self-reported function, and quality of life. Second,
a clinical examination must confirm the presence of tenderness or crepitus (joint popping
sound). Third, the patient should have a Kellgren & Lawrence (KL) grade of 0 or 1.
    These criteria intentionally exclude (Magnetic Resonance Imaging) MRI due to limited
supporting evidence and consensus, which makes the criteria more accessible for primary
healthcare providers by focusing on practical diagnostic elements [3]. Since knee osteoar-
thritis (KOA) progresses over several years, it often starts without noticeable symptoms,
making early diagnosis challenging. As it advances, patients typically experience pain and
mobility issues, and surgical intervention may become necessary [3].
    The Kellgren and Lawrence grading system is the prominent method for classifying
the severity of KOA based on the frontal or Posteroanterior (PA) view of X-ray images.
It assigns a grade from 0 to 4, with higher grades indicating more severe damage to the
knee joint [6, 7].
    KL0 Normal: No evidence of osteoarthritis on X-ray.
    KL1 Doubtful: Minor osteophyte (bone spur) formation and possible joint space
narrowing.
    KL2 Mild: Definite osteophyte formation and some joint space narrowing.
    KL3 Moderate: Moderate multiple osteophytes, definite joint space narrowing, and
some sclerosis (hardening) of bone ends.
    KL4 Severe: Large osteophytes, severe joint space narrowing, sclerosis, and possible
deformity of bone ends.
    KL classification system also maintains ordering information about the severity level
of OA. It can be shown as.
    KL Grade 0 <KL Grade 1 <KL Grade 2 <KL Grade 3 <KL Grade 4
    The grades are ordered increasingly, with grade 0 indicating no osteoarthritis and grade
4 indicating the most severe damage to the knee joint. The grading system is inherently
ordinal because the grades represent a ranking of the severity of the condition rather than
a numerical measurement. This means the difference between grades is not necessarily
equal, and the intervals between grades may vary. For example, the difference between
grade 0 and grade 1 may be smaller than the difference between grade 2 and grade 3.

   It is important to consider the ordinal nature of the grading system when interpreting
X-ray images and making clinical decisions based on the severity of osteoarthritis [2, 7].
   Automated identification of knee osteoarthritis (KOA) stages from X-rays typically
involves two steps: detecting the knee joint and then determining the severity of KOA.
However, an automated system can perform both tasks in a single step, identifying the knee
joint and assessing KOA severity simultaneously without separating the knee joints before-
hand. The purpose of this study is multifaceted. First, we explored transformer-based archi-
tectures for object detection to isolate knee joints. Second, we evaluated the performance
of these transformer-based networks on the task of KOA classification from knee images.
We conducted two experiments: a single-stage model that simultaneously performs knee
joint detection and KOA severity classification, and a two-stage model that first isolates the
knee joint before classifying KOA severity. We then proposed an enhancement method that
utilizes the ordinal nature of KOA grading, comparing the outcomes of standard classifica-
tion with ordinal classification. Finally, we generated heatmaps (visualizations) to empha-
size critical areas of the radiographs, which helps in understanding and building trust in the
model’s predictions.



2 Related work

Deep Learning (DL) systems have shown tremendous potential for image segmentation,
object detection, and image classification tasks [8, 9]. These models can learn complex
features from large amounts of data, improving models’ accuracy and efficiency. Convo-
lutional Neural Networks (CNN) is the most popular DL network that achieves state-of-
the-art results for image classification tasks [8, 9]. These breakthroughs have motivated the
computer vision community to adapt these ImageNet [10] pre-trained foundation models
e.g. VGG [11], RESNET [12], DenseNet [13] for medical image recognition and classifica-
tion tasks. These networks are applied for tumor detection in Magnetic Resonance Imaging
(MRI), pneumonia prediction from X-rays, skin lesions detection in dermatology images,
and blood cell classification tasks [12]. These networks are also successfully applied to the
KOA classification task [14–17].
    In one study, Guida et al. [18] compared the performance of deep learning models for
X-rays and 3D MRI sequences. They performed various experiments for knee OA classifi-
cation using MRI and similar experiments with X-rays. In addition, Multiple experiments
were performed for 5 KL grades and 2 class OA classifications. They also used trans-
fer learning approaches with 2D CNN models such as VGG-16, ResNet, and DenseNet.
Results for MRI assessments showed better results when compared to X-rays. KL grade 2
is the most commonly misclassified.
    Feng et al. [19] presented channel and spatial attention modules to enhance the model’s
performance. In addition, a Mish activation function was used to improve the stability of
the network. A ResNet was customized to reduce the parameters and depth of the network.
Then, a Convolution Block Attention Module (CBAM) is implemented with a Mish acti-
vation Function. Finally, Grad-CAM is used to visualize the output of the network. These
attention modules help filter feature map information, directing focus to crucial regions.
Additionally, the Mish activation function, used instead of ReLU, enhances network stabil-
ity and facilitates faster convergence. Using a convolutional neural network (CNN) archi-
tecture with these attention modules and the Mish activation function, the method achieved
an accuracy of 70.23%, recall of 68.23%, precision of 70.25%, and an F1 score of 67.55%

on the knee osteoarthritis dataset. The model’s performance was inconsistent across all
grades, hence Their method achieved the lowest accuracy for KL grade 1 i.e. 15%.
    In most studies, [20–22] knee joint localization is done by YOLO and their obtained
Intersection Over Union (IOU) is 92.2%, 93%, 95.42% respectively.
    In work by Wang et al. [23], an automated method to extract ROI is suggested. This
ROI is then sent for classification. An object detection model, YOLO, is finetuned to detect
knee joints. For classification, ResNet-50 is the backbone architecture. Finally, Visual
Transformer (ViT) blocks extract features for final classification.
    In another work by Li et al. [24] extracted radiographic features using PA and lateral
X-rays. Multiple experiments were performed using popular machine learning algorithms.
In this study, they used a private dataset of knee X-rays. Both frontal and lateral X-rays
were labeled by radiologists for knee segmentation. Then, radiomic signatures were cre-
ated. The whole datum was randomly grouped into 80% crossvalidation and 20% inde-
pendent testing. Dimensionality reduction was applied using LASSO. The best results were
obtained using LR to differentiate between Normal and KOA knees.
    Alshareef et al.[25] have fine-tuned an ImageNet pre-trained Vision Transformer to
the KOA classification task. They also performed experiments with CNNs and compared
results. GradCAM does feature visualization. However, the authors suggest improvements
using large and diverse datasets. In one study [26] simultaneous knee joint detection and
KL grade classification are applied with the Faster RCNN model. The overall average
accuracy reported by them is 82.5%. A semisupervised deep learning approach is imple-
mented with siamese netwerk by Nguyen et al.[27]. They obtained an average accuracy of
71%. In one work by Tariq et al. [2] have performed various experiments with pre-trained
CNN networks, i.e., ResNet-34, VGG-19, and DenseNet(121 & 161) CNNs for KOA clas-
sification tasks. They have suggested an ordinal classification approach with an ordinal loss
function. They also made an ensemble, improving the KOA classification’s overall and per-
grade accuracy. They have also implemented EigenCAM visualization for a better under-
standing of results.
    In a recent study, Sekhri et al. [28] have used a Swin transformer for the KOA predic-
tion task. They performed experiments on two popular public datasets and implemented a
data drift correction strategy to abridge the gap between them. Finally, they implemented
GradCAM as an interpretability tool to visualize features learned by the model.
    KOA severity level prediction using X-rays is an image classification task in which each
KL grade is assigned a distinct category.
    This is a multiclass classification approach, so a multiple-binary classifier approach can

    Classical deep learning image classification systems treat it as multiclass classification
and train the CNNs using the Cross-Entropy (CE) loss function [19, 30]. In the context of
knee osteoarthritis (KOA) classification, for example, the model might need to classify an
X-ray image into one of several severity levels, such as normal, mild, moderate, or severe.
Each severity level corresponds to a different class, and the model is trained to recognize
patterns in the images that correlate with these specific classes. The CE loss function is
Multimedia Tools and Applications (2025) 84:41353–41380                                 41357


preferred in multiclass classification tasks because it provides a strong theoretical foun-
dation and works well empirically for optimizing classification models. It directly relates
to the concept of maximizing the likelihood of the observed data, making it effective for
learning complex patterns in data. The CE loss function is designed for multiclass classifi-
cation problems where the classes are treated as distinct and unordered categories [31, 32].
This means that the CE loss function assumes that all errors are equally severe, regardless
of the relative ordering or closeness of the predicted and actual classes [2, 3, 19].
   Studies by [20, 33], and [34] applied an ordinal classification approach for the KL grad-
ing task, achieving overall accuracies of 69.7%, 71.08%, and 88.09%, respectively. How-
ever, accuracy across individual KL grades remained inconsistent, with KL grade 1 show-
ing particularly poor prediction results.


2.1 Ressearch gaps

Upon reviewing the literature, we identified the following gaps in research. • KOA severity
is often assessed using the Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) grading system, which is inherently
subjective and can vary significantly between different observers [2, 28]. This variability
is reflected in the reading reliability of the KL grading, where inter-observer agreement
(measured by the Kappa statistic) ranges from 0.56 to 0.67 [35], indicating only moderate
agreement.

• Most of the previous studies treat this problem as a classification task, while the KL
  grading system is ordinal, meaning the grades are ordered from normal (KL0) to severe
  (KL4). However, these grades do not represent equal intervals of disease progression,
  complicating analysis and modeling efforts. This lack of equal spacing between grades
  can make it difficult to accurately capture the nuances of disease severity, which can
  impact clinical decision-making [2, 3].
• Moreover, the KL grading system is challenged by both inter-class and intra-class vari-
  ability. Inter-class variability refers to the differences in severity between different KL
  grades, while intra-class variability refers to the differences in severity within a single
  KL grade. It has been observed that intra-class variability can sometimes be greater
  than inter-class variability. This means that the severity of KOA within a single grade
  (e.g., KL2) can vary more widely than the difference between two consecutive grades
  (e.g., KL2 and KL3). Such variability adds complexity to the interpretation of X-rays
  and the development of automated classification models, as the models must account
  for these inconsistencies to provide reliable and accurate assessments. Addressing both
  intra- and inter-class variability is crucial to improving the robustness of automated
  KOA classification systems and ensuring they can be effectively used in clinical prac-
  tice [3, 36].
• In many previous studies, More confusing KL grades were either removed or merged
  [37]. To overcome this situation, the previous studies tried to solve it as a binary or ter-
  tiary classification problem [7, 24, 38–40].
• The use of deep learning for knee osteoarthritis (KOA) classification from X-rays has
  predominantly relied on convolutional neural networks (CNNs) [17, 19, 34]. These
  CNN-based models have demonstrated strong performance in detecting and classifying
  KOA by learning hierarchical features from medical images. However, with the recent
  development of vision transformer networks, which have shown impressive results
  in various computer vision tasks [41, 42], there has been growing interest in apply-
41358                                        Multimedia Tools and Applications (2025) 84:41353–41380


   ing these models to KOA classification using X-rays. Only a few studies have so far
   explored the use of transformers for this purpose [25, 28]. This study aims to evalu-
   ate the effectiveness and suitability of various transformer architectures for classifying
   KOA from X-ray images, providing insights into how these models perform compared
   to traditional CNN-based approaches.

2.2 Our contributions

In this study, we evaluated the application of transformer networks to localize knee joints
and classify knee osteoarthritis.

• We applied an object detection transformer for knee joint localization. This study is one
  of its kind to consider multiple data sources for knee joint detection.
• We converted the KL grading format of four different datasets to COCO JSON format.
  we also generated knee joint images with KL grades for three datasets.
• We implemented a single-stage knee joint localization with KOA classification accord-
  ing to the KL grading system.
• We explored Vision Transformers (ViT), Swin Transformers, Pyramid Vision Trans-
  formers (PCPVT), and Dual Attention ViT (DaViT) and compared the multiclass clas-
  sification results.
• We suggested a mechanism to improve the prediction performance of these networks
  using an ordinal classification approach that fits the knee osteoarthritis x-ray classifica-
  tion task well.
• We have also compared the results of multiclass classification vs ordinal image classifi-
  cation approach using different loss functions.
• Finally, we implemented an explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) approach to help
  interpret and visualize the features extracted by our best model and to improve trans-
  parency.
• We also validated our best models across multiple datasets.
• This study is unique in its implementation as it covers a wide range of localization and
  classification transformers and compares their results for Knee X-rays. It also takes
  into consideration the implicit ordinal nature of KL grades and provides stateof-the-art
  results for all KL grades.

3.2 Preprocessing

For the development of our knee joint localization and knee osteoarthritis (KOA) clas-
sification models, we curated training, testing, and validation datasets from two primary
sources: the OAI (Osteoarthritis Initiative) and CHECK (Cohort Hip and Cohort Knee)
studies. These datasets provided knee radiographs but did not include any existing annota-
tions for knee joint localization. To address this, we developed custom labeling software,
which allowed us to manually annotate the knee joints in each radiograph. Through this
process, we created the essential ground truth data for knee joint detection, ensuring that
each annotated region represented an accurate bounding box around the knee joint. The
annotations were then converted into COCO-JSON format, a standard in computer vision,
to facilitate their use in training both the knee localization and classification models.
    After our knee joint localization model demonstrated strong accuracy, we utilized it
to detect knee joints in radiographs from additional datasets, specifically the CGMH-OA
and G&A datasets. By incorporating these new detections, we assembled a mixed dataset
that enabled cross-dataset validation of our best transformer-based classifier, enhancing its
robustness across different sources of radiographic data. To streamline data preparation,
we also created software capable of automatically cropping the knee joint regions based on
the bounding boxes generated by our localization model, along with their associated KL
grades (ranging from KL0 to KL4). To ensure consistency across datasets, this software
also resized the cropped images, preserving a uniform physical resolution and enabling the
images to be directly comparable across studies.
    To enhance the diversity of the dataset and improve the model’s generalizability, we
employed a range of image augmentation techniques. These augmentations included
adjustments to brightness and saturation levels, which simulate variations in Xray
exposure and contrast, allowing the model to adapt to images from different imaging
41360                                       Multimedia Tools and Applications (2025) 84:41353–41380


equipment or settings. Horizontal flipping was also applied to help the model generalize
to variations in patient positioning.
    In addition, random affine transformations, such as rotations, translations, and scal-
ing, were used to create variations in image orientation and scale. This is particularly
useful given that knee X-ray images often display differences in leg alignment due to
conditions like severe knee deformity, which can result in images where the legs are not
straight. Finally, normalization was implemented to standardize pixel intensity distribu-
tions across images, reducing the influence of lighting discrepancies. Together, these
transformations ensure that the model is exposed to a broad spectrum of imaging condi-
tions and knee presentations, enhancing its robustness and ability to accurately classify
KOA across diverse clinical scenarios.
    Despite the advantages of a mixed dataset, we faced certain limitations. The Kaggle
CGMH-OA and G&A datasets, for example, contained relatively few samples, and the
CHECK dataset lacked examples with the KL4 grade. Consequently, these datasets were
not ideal for training a Vision Transformer (ViT), which requires ample samples in each
class to achieve stable learning. Nonetheless, we used this mixed dataset effectively for
fine-tuning and cross-dataset validation of our top-performing transformer-based clas-
sifier. This approach allowed us to validate the classifier’s performance across diverse
radiographic datasets, strengthening its reliability and potential for broader clinical
application.

3.3 Networks

Transformer networks comprising encoder-decoder architecture are based only on an
attention mechanism. Transformers were first introduced for natural language process-
ing tasks that included language translation [48]. The Transformer Encoder Block con-
sists of Norm, Multi-head Attention, and (Multi Layer Perceptron) MLP. Where Layer
Norm helps normalize the distributions of intermediate layers. It enables smoother
gradients, faster training, and better generalization accuracy. Multihead Self-Attention
(MSA) is an extension of Self-Attention (SA). It concatenates all the attention outputs.


3.3.1 Detection transformer (DETR)

Detection Transformer is an object detection deep learning model [49]. Its main com-
ponent is a Transformer. DETR depends on a CNN backbone that extracts high-level
features from the image. Spatial information about the objects in the image is added by
DETR and fed to the Transformer Encoder. DETR introduces the concept of object que-
ries, keys, and values. The number of object queries is already known and can vary from
image to image. Keys are the spatial location of an object in an image, while values are
the extracted features that represent that object. MSA helps capture and maintain infor-
mation about object relationships and their dependencies in an image, hence keeping the
information about an object’s local and global contexts.


3.3.2 Visiont transformer (ViT)

Vision Transformer, or ViT, is a state-of-the-art deep learning model in computer vision.
It was introduced in 2020 as an alternative to the popular CNN architecture [41]. The
Vision Transformer model uses a self-attention mechanism to capture global dependen-
cies between image patches, which helps it perform well on tasks such as image classifi-
cation, object detection, and segmentation. The model is trained on large-scale datasets
such as ImageNet and achieves state-of-the-art results with fewer parameters than tradi-
tional CNN-based models.
   ViT works on global image information. It splits the images into small patches,
vectorizes them, and extracts contextual data of an image on a patch-by-patch basis.
Transformer’s attention mechanism helps in learning contextual information, which is
helpful in image classification tasks. These patches and details of a class token are fed
into Multi Head Spatial Attention (MSA) and output the learned embeddings of patches.
Lastly, an MLP is used to classify the learned image representations.


3.3.3 Swin transformer (Swin)

Swin is another image classification model based on the conventional transformer
[42]. The input image is converted to a series of patches. A patch size of 4 × 4 pro-
duces a feature dimension of 4 × 4 × 3 = 48 that is then passed to the linear embedding
layer. Each Swin block consists of a layer norm (LN), W-MSA, and SW-MSA, multi-
head self-attention modules with regular and shifted windowing configurations. Swin
41362                                          Multimedia Tools and Applications (2025) 84:41353–41380


Transformer divides the image into stages, each consisting of windows. This hierarchi-
cal approach allows the model to capture information at different levels of granularity.


3.3.4 PCPVT

PCPVT is a Pyramid Vision Transformer that uses conditional positional encoding,
unlike PVT but has the same number of parameters and FLOPS [50]. This network has
achieved state-of-the-art results in image classification tasks and has more than 61 million
parameters.


3.3.5 Dual attention vision transformer (DaViT)

DaVit is based on two types of attention and tokens. First is “spatial window selfattention
with spatial tokens” and channel group “self-attention with channel tokens” [51]. The two
forms of attention are arranged as complementary and computationally efficient.

3.4.2 Single‑stage knee joint localization and KOA classification

The second approach uses a single-stage object detection and localization model. That

3.4.3 Two‑stage knee joint localization and KOA classification

We implemented vision transformer models for the KOA classification in the last approach.
Under this approach, various ImageNet pre-trained Vision transformers were applied and com-
pared using transfer learning. We used knee joint images already detected by the knee-joint
localization model. We fine-tuned ViT, SwinViT, PCPVT, and DaViT. At this stage, models
were trained with Cross Entropy loss. To train the ViT classifiers effectively, we used a batch
size of 28 for each training iteration. To enhance the model’s ability to generalize across vari-
ations in radiographs, we applied a sequence of image transformations to each batch. These
transformations, chained together, included adjustments to brightness and saturation, horizon-
tal flipping, random affine transformations, and normalization. This approach was designed to
diversify the input data, making the model more robust to variations in imaging conditions.
Once these transformations were applied, the batch was fed into the model for training.

3.5.9 ANOVA test

The Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) test is used to evaluate whether the differences in mean
values among two or more groups are statistically significant, based on sample data. Specifi-
cally, a one-way ANOVA test assesses whether the observed variation in group means reflects
an actual difference between the groups or if it could simply be attributed to random variation
within each group. In ANOVA, the F statistic plays a crucial role as it represents the ratio of
two types of variance: the variance between the group means (i.e., the variability due to the
differences among the groups) and the variance within the groups (i.e., the variability within
each group itself). This ratio helps in determining if the observed differences among group
means are large enough to be considered significant. A smaller F statistic suggests that the
group means are likely similar, indicating no significant difference, while a larger F statistic
suggests a greater likelihood that the group means are not equal, thus pointing to a potential
significant difference.

4.4 Ordinal classification

It was observed that models make wrong predictions for adjacent grades, i.e., KL1 needs
to be clarified with KL0 and KL2. To improve these models’ performances, we replaced
the loss function with Conditional Ordinal Regression for neural Networks implemented in
work [2].

4.4.1 Results

Models’ performance before and after the change of loss function can also be observed.
After modifying the model, No individual accuracy was below 80% except for ViTOrd
KL = 1 and KL = 2. As mentioned earlier, Cross-Entropy (CE) loss does not take into
account the ordinal nature of the classes when classifying images, which can lead to subop-
timal performance. However, after applying an ordinal approach that considers the inherent
order of the classes, the performance has significantly improved.

4.4.2 Interpretation

A comparison of all the models with Cross Entropy Loss and Ordinal Loss is shown in
Fig. 3. The results indicate that the ViTOrd model achieved a minimum accuracy of 83%,

entropy (CE) loss. This improvement is also reflected in the Kappa statistic, which reached
0.92, highlighting a strong agreement between the predicted and actual classifications.
    Similarly, the Swin model, when trained with ordinal loss, showed significant perfor-

the other models across all metrics, with the highest Accuracy (93%), Precision (94%),

4.5 Eigen CAM

EigenCAM is a type of class activation mapping (CAM) method that uses the principal
components of the gradients flowing back from the output layer to the feature maps of
a convolutional layer. It identifies the most important features (image regions) that influ-
ence the model’s decision, typically visualized as a heatmap overlaid on the original image.
EigenCAM visualizations of our best model, i.e., DaViTOrd, are shown in Table 9. The
model could highlight key areas to help medical practitioners identify knee joint narrow-
ing, sclerosis, and osteophytes in yellow and blue. It is worth mentioning that the area
highlighted by the CAM indicates the significant region for potential detection of KOA
symptoms and can be utilized for precise assignment of the KL grade.
